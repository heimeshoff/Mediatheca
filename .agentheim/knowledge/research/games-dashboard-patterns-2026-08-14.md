# Research: Video-game-tracking dashboard patterns and backlog decision-making

## Question
What do the best-loved video-game-tracking and game-library applications show on their dashboard/home screens, and which of those features do users actually praise or heavily use? Applied to: redesigning the Games dashboard of a single-user, event-sourced media library app that already has play sessions (dates+durations), personal ratings, an "in focus" shortlist, and friend/social metadata (recommended-by, want-to-play-with, played-with).

## Summary

- **Recording ("what have I played") and deciding ("what should I play now") are different jobs, and the most-loved apps are good at the first and bad at the second.** Backloggd's own power-user reviewer, after six months, concluded it's "a museum of your collection" that "excels at recording history but fails at decision-making" — it offers no help when you actually sit down with limited time [7].
- **Status buckets converge on the same five-ish states across every tool** (Playing / Backlog-Wishlist / Completed / Abandoned / Shelved-On Hold), but two categories keep getting requested and bolted on afterward because the default sets don't fit reality: an explicit **"Abandoned"** state distinct from "never started" [4], and a state for **games that can't be "beaten"** (endless/live-service/roguelike) — requested as "Endless"/"Unbeatable"/"Infinite" on Playnite years after launch, still unresolved as a first-class default [5].
- **Time-to-beat data (HowLongToBeat) is loved as a rough sizing signal, not trusted as a precise estimate.** Cross-checked complaints: small sample sizes per game, wide variance in submissions (2h–12h for the same title), and users treating it as "how big is this game" rather than "how long will *I* take" [12]. Its "connect your library, see total backlog hours" feature is specifically called out by users as a "killer feature" for backlog awareness [8], despite the underlying per-game numbers being fuzzy.
- **Algorithmic "play next" recommendation shelves (Steam) get lukewarm reviews even when technically working.** Steam's ML-based Play Next surfaces games you own but haven't played; reception is mixed-to-negative — it over-indexes on well-known/bundle-leftover titles, doesn't refresh well, and users have asked for a "just shuffle/randomize" fallback instead of trusting the algorithm [2][3][13].
- **Manual random pickers ("spin the wheel", Steam Roulette, pickaga.me) are a whole cottage-industry response to backlog choice paralysis** — evidence that algorithmic curation isn't solving the "what do I play tonight" problem and people are filling the gap themselves with filterable randomizers (by genre, co-op, playtime, price) [16].
- **Friction kills logging habits.** The single most concrete causal claim found: a Backloggd user's nightly-logging habit died specifically because mobile entry took ~45 seconds vs. ~10 seconds on a dedicated app, and lack of import meant manually re-entering an entire library (abandoned partway through, at letter "G") [7]. This is a strong argument for low-friction capture in a single-user app that already has structured event data — the win is *not requiring extra logging effort* on top of what's already tracked.
- **Choice paralysis is named and studied (Barry Schwartz's "paradox of choice") and gets worse, not better, as libraries grow** via subscription services — more options increases perceived stakes of the choice rather than satisfaction [14]. No community tool fully solves it; ad hoc practices (2-hour minimum before dropping, running one story-heavy + one casual game in parallel, capping "1 new for every 2 backlog" purchases) are folk remedies, not tool features [17].

## Findings

### 1. Backloggd — the social/journal model
Backloggd (650k+ registered users, 31.5M games logged, 4.8M journal entries in 2025 alone [1]) is explicitly positioned as "Letterboxd for games." Its logged-in home page centers on an **activity feed** — "games, reviews, and lists from friends" — with status buckets (Playing, Completed, Abandoned, Shelved, Wishlist) organizing the personal collection, plus journaling, time logging, platform-ownership tagging, and a 5-point (with half-steps) rating system [6].

What's praised: the interface itself ("clearly superior to competitors like HowLongToBeat"), turning a grid of cover art into something users *enjoy* looking at rather than something anxiety-inducing, a genuinely active community (curated lists, thoughtful reviews), and that every feature is free with no paywalled tier [7][1]. Users specifically credit the developer's roadmap and responsiveness [1].

What's complained about: missing/obscure titles (especially Japanese digital-only games) requiring manual addition [1]; account-activation/email friction on signup [1]; and — the big one — **no import tooling**, forcing manual re-entry of an entire back catalog, and **mobile logging friction** high enough to kill a nightly-logging habit within months [7]. Crucially, even a fan concludes it does nothing to help you pick what to play *right now* [7].

Takeaway for a single-user app: Backloggd's core loved surface (clean grid, simple status buckets, journal) is worth emulating for the "collection" view, but its central home-page metaphor — a social activity feed — is meaningless in a single-user context and shouldn't be copied. The "museum, not decision-maker" critique is the single most load-bearing finding for this redesign.

### 2. HowLongToBeat — time-to-beat as backlog currency
HLTB's core value proposition is turning your backlog into a **time budget**: Main Story / Main+Extra / Completionist estimates per game, and (via Steam library connection) a personal stat converting your whole backlog into total hours — sometimes paired with a "will you live long enough to finish it" framing (the "Steam Survival Calculator" is a widely-circulated companion tool built on HLTB data) [9]. Users describe comparing time-to-beat across backlog games as a "killer feature" for triage, and the site supports multiple named lists (e.g., "modern backlog," "retro backlog," "replays") [8].

Accuracy is consistently second-guessed: per-game submission counts can be tiny (under ~100 for less popular titles) with wide spread (a single game showing 2h and 12h submissions) [12]. Cross-source agreement: at least three independent threads (ResetEra, a personal blog, GameFAQs) converge on "useful as a rough size gauge, not a personal prediction" [12]. HLTB itself acknowledges limits by excluding multiplayer-only and "endless" titles from its estimates entirely [website could not be fetched directly; corroborated via secondary source WindowsClub 9].

Takeaway: the *feature* people love isn't the precision, it's the **conversion of "how many games" into "how many hours"** — a reframing that makes backlog size legible. Given this app already has session durations recorded (not estimates — *actuals*), it's in a stronger position than HLTB for its own played history, and could use estimates (if added) purely for *unplayed* backlog sizing, clearly labeled as rough.

### 3. Steam — library home, Play Next, Replay
Steam's rebuilt library home is shelf-based: **Recent** (played/purchased most recently — praised by at least one user as "topmost, everything at reach of a click," but limited to ~2 weeks of history, a repeated complaint) [10], **Updates**, **Friends' Activity**, and user-defined **Custom Shelves** built from drag-and-drop or dynamic tag/filter collections [11].

**Play Next** is an ML shelf recommending owned-but-unplayed/barely-played titles based on "players like you." Reception is genuinely mixed: Kotaku's reviewer found it "pretty good" for surfacing genre-appropriate picks (tactics/RPG) but noted it skewed almost entirely toward well-known titles, with "only one game on my whole list I'd even describe as slightly obscure" [2]. Steam Community threads independently corroborate the "generic list of bundle leftovers," duplicate-edition recommendations (e.g. suggesting a game you already own a different edition of), and a lack of refresh/reshuffle control, with users requesting a manual randomize/shuffle fallback instead [3]. Two independent sources (Kotaku editorial, Steam Community user threads) agree the algorithm's core failure mode is "confident but generic," not "broken."

**Steam Replay** (year-in-review) draws mixed but largely positive emotional engagement, undercut by two recurring, independently-corroborated technical complaints: a data cutoff that excludes the back half of December (missing the holiday gaming surge) [3rd source], and outright breakage for users with private profiles [source: SteamCommunity threads]. The "fun retrospective, annoying execution" pattern echoes generally-loved *year-in-review* features in other domains (Spotify Wrapped, Letterboxd Year) — the appetite for a stats retrospective is real even when execution frustrates.

Achievement showcases are the most polarizing surface found: genuinely valued by completionists as proof of effort, but undermined community-wide by the knowledge that achievement-unlocker tools (SAM) make earned vs. faked indistinguishable, and by achievements carrying no other value on the platform (no leveling, etc.) [15]. Weak signal for this app: achievement-style badges are not a strongly-loved pattern absent a way to make them feel "earned" and meaningful, and are probably not worth dashboard real estate.

### 4. Playnite / GOG Galaxy — self-hosted / unified-launcher patterns
Playnite (open-source, plugin-extensible, no cloud account required) is the closest structural analog to a self-hosted single-user tracker. Its completion-status model is the most instructive data point in this research: the **default set is small and has proven insufficient in the field**. Multiple long-running GitHub feature requests document the gaps:
- Users wanted, and eventually got, a distinct **"Abandoned"** status specifically for "when you decide to never come back to a game" — separate from simply never having started it [4].
- A status for games that structurally **can't be "beaten"** (endless, live-service, roguelike/roguelite) is still an open request years in — proposed names include "Endless," "Unbeatable," "Infinite" — with the request driven partly by theme/iconography needs (visual differentiation matters to users, not just the label) [5].
- Users have separately asked for the ability to fully **customize/add/remove statuses** rather than wait on new fixed presets from the maintainers, i.e., people's real mental models of "state of a game" don't collapse into one universal taxonomy [source: GitHub #1388].

GOG Galaxy's pitch is **library unification** (Steam/Epic/GOG/EA/Battle.net in one place) plus per-game detail pages combining achievements, playtime, and cross-platform friend activity; its home surfaces recently-played and friends' milestones [source: GOG support docs, corroborated by secondary GOG Galaxy guide]. Because it targets multi-launcher PC libraries, its dashboard problem (unify scattered data) doesn't map directly onto a single event-sourced app that already owns all its own data — but the "per-game detail = achievements + playtime + activity in one card" pattern is a reasonable template for a game detail page.

Takeaway: **don't ship a fixed, closed status taxonomy.** The two most-requested additions in Playnite's history (Abandoned-as-distinct-from-unstarted, and an endless/live-service state) map exactly onto two of the problem statements in the brief. Build them in from day one rather than bolt them on.

### 5. Grouvee, Infinite Backlog, Ryot — smaller/self-hosted trackers
**Grouvee**: default shelves (Playing/Played/Backlog/Wishlist) plus unlimited user-defined custom shelves (e.g., "co-op with my partner," "summer 2026") [1st search]. Notably, secondary review coverage states Grouvee "doesn't place much emphasis on backlog statistics" — its stats are just shelf counts shown in parentheses, not a dedicated dashboard [source: search synthesis, single-source, not independently verified — flagged as low-confidence]. It does show average community completion time per game and lets users log personal hours (auto-imported from Steam where possible).

**Infinite Backlog**: built on IGDB metadata, praised on ResetEra in thread title alone ("may well be the best app for managing game logs/collection" — full thread content could not be fetched, HTTP 403, so this is a single weakly-verified headline claim, not confirmed detail). Distinguishing features per secondary sources: heavier automation (auto-detects new library additions), achievement tracking, DLC progression tracking, and paywalled custom tags (Patreon-gated) [search synthesis].

**Ryot** (self-hosted, open-source, explicitly the closest architectural sibling to this app — local data, GPLv3, cross-media not just games): dashboard emphasis is on **consolidated statistics/graphs across all tracked media types**, custom collections, saved filter presets, and reminders for "when you want to revisit" an item. Notably it does *not* appear to foreground per-game achievement or completion-percentage detail on its marketing/dashboard pages — the emphasis is macro (trends across your whole media life) rather than micro (per-game deep stats) [source: ryot.io/features, single-source — official vendor copy, treat claims about "AI-driven recommendations" as marketing language, flagged accordingly].

**Ryot/Infinite Backlog/Grouvee could not be cross-checked as thoroughly as Backloggd/Steam/Playnite** — smaller communities produce less independent commentary. Treat findings from this section as directionally useful but lower-confidence than sections 1–4.

### 6. Backlog-management culture (r/patientgamers and adjacent communities)
The "paradox of choice" (Barry Schwartz) is explicitly invoked by multiple independent writers to explain why bigger libraries produce *more* anxiety, not more satisfaction — subscription services (Game Pass, PS Plus) are named as accelerants because they inflate the option set without inflating available time, and larger option sets paradoxically make each individual choice feel higher-stakes [14]. This is a well-established psychology concept applied to games repeatedly across at least four independently-authored articles found in this search (SlashGear, Pietriots, Rice Digital, Two Average Gamers) — treat the underlying psychology claim as reasonably solid, the game-specific application as journalistic consensus rather than academic study.

Community-generated coping mechanisms, cross-referenced across multiple sources: a personal rule of a minimum time commitment before allowing yourself to drop a game (commonly "2 hours") [17]; deliberately running one demanding/story-heavy game in parallel with one lighter/casual game so there's always a fit for the current mood/energy level [17]; self-imposed purchase ratios ("finish two backlog games for every one new purchase") [17]; and organized "backlog challenges"/"Backlog Assassins" communities that gamify burn-down with public commitment (Steam group "attackyourbacklog," "Operation Backlog Completion" blog series) [search synthesis, multiple corroborating but low-authority sources].

The clearest *tooling* response to choice paralysis is the proliferation of **randomizer/wheel pickers** — at least six distinct tools found (spinthewheel.app variants, Steam Roulette, Random Game Generator, pickaga.me, Random Game Picker, Xbox Game Pass Game Picker) — several explicitly filterable by **genre, co-op support, playtime, and price**, i.e., by the practical constraints of "what fits my evening" [16]. This is strong triangulated evidence that (a) people want a *decide-for-me* mechanism, not just a browsable list, and (b) the filter that matters most in practice is *time/session-length available*, not genre or rating.

### 7. Endless/live-service games and abandoned-but-not-dropped titles
This is the least-served case across every tool surveyed. HowLongToBeat's estimate model **structurally excludes** multiplayer-only and endless games rather than modeling them [9]. Playnite's community has been asking for a first-class "can't be beaten" status for years without it landing as a default [5]. "Abandoned" as a state distinct from "never started" was itself a multi-year feature request before being added [4] — meaning even the *concept* of "I gave up, on purpose, and that's a valid terminal state" wasn't native to the earliest tools. Smaller/niche trackers (Keep Track of My Games) explicitly market flexible statuses (Abandoned/Beaten/100% Completed) as a differentiator, suggesting the market recognizes this as an unsolved gap rather than a solved-and-forgotten one [search synthesis].

## Patterns: single-user/self-hosted apps vs. social platforms

- **Social platforms (Backloggd, Steam, GOG Galaxy) default their home screen to other people's activity.** That's structurally unavailable and undesirable in a single-user app — the home screen real estate that a social feed would occupy needs to be replaced with something else, not left blank. The friend-data this app *does* have (recommended-by, want-to-play-with, played-with) is a much better fit for **contextual metadata on individual game cards** ("recommended by X," "you and Y both want to play this") than for a feed.
- **Self-hosted/local tools (Playnite, Ryot) lean toward statistics/graphs and flexible taxonomy rather than social proof**, because there's no community layer to lean on. Ryot's emphasis on cross-media trend graphs over per-item deep stats is a useful signal: aggregate "state of my backlog" views (counts per status, hours logged this month/year) are a self-hosted-appropriate substitute for the social engagement loop that public trackers rely on.
- **Fixed status taxonomies break in the field, repeatedly, in the same two places** (distinct Abandoned state; a state for unbeatable/endless games) [4][5] — this app should design those in from the start rather than treat status as a small fixed enum.
- **No tool surveyed successfully merges "record what I did" with "help me decide what to do now" in one coherent interface** — Backloggd's own fan explicitly names this as the app's central failure [7]. This is the single clearest opportunity: an app that already has *actual* session history (not self-reported estimates) and an "in focus" shortlist is unusually well positioned to build the decision-support half that every competitor is missing.

## What actually helps "what should I play tonight" (session-length-aware picking)

Cross-referencing sections 3, 6, and the catalog table, the elements that show real (if partial) evidence of helping this specific decision are:

1. **Filtering/picking by available time is a named, explicit axis in third-party tools** (pickaga.me sorts by playtime/HLTB-vs-actual-playtime; Steam Roulette filters by playtime; wheel tools let you build separate "solo night" vs "group session" wheels) [16]. None of the major trackers (Backloggd, Grouvee, Playnite) build a time-available filter into their own status/shelf model — it's exclusively a third-party-tool concern. This is a gap this app could fill natively, since it already has actual per-session duration data to calibrate against (rather than relying on unverified crowd-sourced HLTB numbers).
2. **A narrowed, curated shortlist beats a browsable full library for reducing choice paralysis** — indirectly evidenced by the paradox-of-choice literature (bigger option sets increase anxiety, not satisfaction) [14] and directly evidenced by the existence and popularity of hand-curated "in progress"/"recently played" shelves over full-library browsing on Steam [10]. This app's existing "in focus" shortlist concept is aligned with what the research supports — narrow, not exhaustive, framing.
3. **Randomization-with-constraints is a validated pattern users build for themselves when tools don't provide it** — worth treating as a legitimate low-effort dashboard feature (e.g., "surprise me from my in-focus list, given N minutes") rather than a novelty, given how many independent third-party tools exist purely to fill this gap [16].
4. **A distinct decision axis for "long deep-dive" vs "short session" games is not native to any surveyed tool** — no tracker tags games by typical session shape (settle-in RPG vs. 20-minute roguelike run vs. quick match). This is unclaimed territory; if this app tagged games (or inferred from session-length history, which it already logs) it would be doing something none of the surveyed competitors do natively.
5. **Time-to-beat framing (hours-to-finish, backlog-hours-total) is loved as an awareness tool, not a decision tool** — it tells you the backlog is big, not what to play in the next hour. Don't conflate the two: a "your backlog = 400 hours" stat satisfies curiosity/anxiety-naming but doesn't answer tonight's question, per both the HLTB accuracy-skepticism findings [12] and the Backloggd "museum not decision-maker" critique [7].

## Open questions

- **HowLongToBeat's own site could not be directly fetched** (WebFetch blocked/unavailable for howlongtobeat.com); all HLTB claims here are secondary-sourced through at least two independent outlets each, which raises confidence somewhat, but a first-party look at their actual profile/stats UI would still be valuable if this becomes a serious reference point for the redesign.
- **Grouvee's and Infinite Backlog's actual dashboards were not directly viewed** (ResetEra thread on Infinite Backlog returned HTTP 403; Grouvee stats claims come from a single secondary blog whose direct fetch also failed) — treat section 5 as the weakest-evidenced part of this report and revisit with direct screenshots/trials if these two specifically become design references.
- **No quantitative usage data** (e.g., "% of Steam users who ever open Play Next" or "% of Backloggd users who maintain a journal past month 1") was found for any feature in this report — all "loved/used" claims are qualitative (reviews, forum threads, community sentiment), not telemetry. Framing in the summary reflects this: claims are about *sentiment*, not *measured adoption*.
- **Simkl-for-games** (mentioned in the original brief) turned up no distinct, substantive coverage in this search pass — Simkl's game-tracking features (if any) were not found separately from its TV/movie tracking identity and were not investigated further; flagged as unresearched rather than assumed absent.
- **No source directly addressed "abandoned-but-not-dropped"** as a state distinct from both "abandoned" and "playing" (e.g., a live-service game you check in on for a few hours every few months, never really "done," never really "quit") — the closest analog found is the endless/live-service completion-status gap in Playnite (#5), but that's about games with no ending, not about intermittent-but-ongoing engagement specifically. This may be a genuinely novel design problem not yet solved by any surveyed tool.
